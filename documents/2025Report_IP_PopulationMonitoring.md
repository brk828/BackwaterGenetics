# Population Monitoring — Imperial Ponds California (IPCA)

**B. Kesner — Marsh & Associates**

## Imperial Ponds — Razorback Sucker

### Background

Razorback Sucker (RASU; *Xyrauchen texanus*) were initially stocked at three of the six Imperial Ponds California (IPCA) backwaters — Ponds 1, 3, and 4 — in fiscal year 2017. Monitoring of PIT-tagged RASU at these ponds uses the same approach as Yuma Cove Backwater (YCB): continuous passive PIT scanning supplemented by periodic netting and trapping events. The results presented here draw on PIT scanning data from 2016 through 2025 and encompass all stocking and capture events recorded in the Lower Colorado River Native Fishes Database for these locations.

---

### Post-Stocking Survival

Detection probability for RASU stocked at IPCA Ponds 1, 3, and 4 was significantly dependent on sex, release total length (TL), and pond (Table A). The model was fit across all three XYTE ponds simultaneously with pond included as a fixed predictor to account for differences in scanner coverage, pond geometry, and local conditions that could influence detection rates independent of fish characteristics. The positive effect of TL on detection probability was consistent with patterns observed at YCB: larger fish at release were more likely to be detected beyond the 90-day post-stocking window (Figure A). Female RASU were released at larger average TL than males across all ponds and stocking years, contributing to higher apparent post-stocking survival among females in most stocking events (Table B). This TL discrepancy between sexes is a characteristic of the production pipeline that supplies fish to these backwaters and is not unexpected given the typical size at sexual maturity for each sex.

Differences in detection probability among ponds, as estimated by the model coefficients (Table A), reflect a combination of factors including scanner configuration, the proportion of the pond surveyed by passive antennas, and variation in stocking timing relative to scanner availability. These pond-level differences should not be interpreted as differences in true survival probability without additional data on scanner effectiveness at each location.

Overall post-stocking detection rates at IPCA XYTE ponds were variable by year and pond (Table B). As at YCB, male detection rates were consistently lower than those of females in most stocking events, and survival declined in subsequent stockings relative to the initial 2017 cohort — a pattern attributable in part to the smaller average TL of fish in later stockings. Fish stocked at or below approximately 350 mm TL showed notably lower detection probability in the model, consistent with the view that smaller fish face greater post-release vulnerability.

### Known Survivors

The minimum known survivor counts for RASU at each IPCA pond reflect the cumulative effect of initial and supplemental stocking events plus natural recruitment, reduced by natural attrition tracked through passive PIT scanning (Figure B). Each stocking event appears as a step increase in the survivor trajectory, followed by a decline as fish are progressively lost to mortality or extended absence from scanner range. Ponds 1, 3, and 4 each show a distinct stocking and attrition history, and differences among ponds are expected given variation in stocking dates, cohort sizes, and scanner coverage across locations.

Natural recruitment into the PIT-tagged population was highly variable among XYTE ponds. Pond 1 had substantial and sustained natural recruitment, with 242 recruits that survived the initial 90-day post-tagging period across seven cohort years (2017–2023). Recruitment at Pond 1 was first detected in 2017 and grew substantially in subsequent sampling years, peaking at 70 recruits in 2022. In contrast, Ponds 3 and 4 had negligible recruitment into the PIT-tagged population — only 5 and 11 surviving recruits, respectively, were recorded across four scattered years (2018, 2020, 2021/2022, and 2023). These fish were first encountered at sizes well below the minimum stocking TL for this species (median stocking TL ≈ 384 mm; most recruits were 100–300 mm at first capture), confirming their origin as naturally reproduced fish tagged during routine sampling rather than stocked adults.

As at YCB, the sex-specific survivor counts illustrate the persistent male deficit that arises from lower post-stocking detection rates among stocked fish. In ponds where supplemental stockings have occurred, the relative contribution of newer cohorts to the living population is evident in the timing of count increases. Fish with confirmed transfer records were excluded from the known survivor analysis; a small number of fish with NFWG transfer records but sustained post-transfer PIT contacts at the same pond were retained following the criteria established for this analysis (see Methods).

The disparity in natural recruitment between Pond 1 and Ponds 3 and 4 is notable and mirrors the pattern documented at YCB, where Pond 1 appears to provide more suitable spawning or nursery conditions, or receives more intensive sampling effort sufficient to detect and tag juveniles. The known survivor trajectory serves as a conservative lower bound on the true living PIT-tagged population; fish that remain in the pond but are consistently missed by passive antennas would not contribute to the known survivor count.

### Population Estimates

Annual modified Petersen mark-recapture population estimates were computed for IPCA XYTE ponds in years where the number of recaptures exceeded three (Figure C). In several census years, particularly in ponds with smaller stocked populations, recapture rates were insufficient to generate estimates, and the known survivor count serves as the only quantitative indicator of population size for those years.

Where estimates were possible, they generally tracked the concurrent known survivor trajectory closely, consistent with the pattern observed at YCB and providing mutual validation between the two methods (Figure C). Estimates with wide confidence intervals reflect census years with low recapture rates (R approaching the minimum threshold of 3) and should be interpreted cautiously. The agreement between the Petersen estimates and known survivor counts in years with adequate recaptures supports the conclusion that the known survivor analysis is capturing a large proportion of the true PIT-tagged population.

In ponds where the population has declined to very low levels, the mark-recapture estimator becomes unreliable regardless of the recapture criterion, and management decisions in those ponds should weight the known survivor count more heavily. The population trajectories for Ponds 3 and 4 are consistent with a pattern of steady post-stocking attrition with negligible natural recruitment into the PIT-tagged population. Pond 1 diverges from this pattern due to sustained natural recruitment (242 surviving recruits across 2017–2023), and its trajectory more closely parallels YCB, where the PIT-tagged population has been substantially augmented by naturally reproduced fish over time.

### Reproductive Contribution — IPCA Pond 1

Parentage assignment data provided by Dowling and Turner (this report) were available for IPCA Pond 1 only; Ponds 3 and 4 lack parentage data at this time and are excluded from this analysis. Results are presented for the 2016 initial stocking cohort (FY2017) through 2022; years beyond that range had insufficient parentage assignments to support reliable proportion estimates.

A portion of surviving male and female RASU stocked in Pond 1 contributed to detectable offspring during the early years following initial stocking (Figure D). As at YCB, reproductive contribution was not uniformly distributed among survivors — some individuals appear to have contributed consistently across multiple years while others made no detectable contribution in any year. The proportion of survivors with detected offspring generally declined over time in both sexes, consistent with the declining overall number of survivors and, potentially, increasing age-related reproductive senescence in the original stocking cohort.

The stocking year counts shown on the figure (numbers of known survivors present in February of each year) underscore that declining proportions in later years are based on small samples, and interpretation should be tempered accordingly. Parentage data for IPCA Ponds 3 and 4 will be incorporated when provided by Dowling and Turner.

## Imperial Ponds — Bonytail

### Background

Bonytail (*Gila elegans*; BONY) were initially stocked at three IPCA backwaters — Ponds 2, 5, and 6 — in fiscal year 2017, concurrent with the RASU stockings at the XYTE ponds. Additional stocking events have occurred in subsequent years at some ponds. As with RASU, monitoring relies on continuous passive PIT scanning and periodic capture events. No parentage data are currently available for Bonytail at any IPCA pond; the reproductive contribution analysis is not included for this species.

---

### Post-Stocking Survival

Post-stocking survival for Bonytail at IPCA Ponds 2, 5, and 6 was modeled for the 2017 initial stocking cohort only, due to insufficient sex data or inadequate post-release scanning windows in subsequent stocking events. Sex, release TL, and pond were included as fixed predictors. The TL range for Bonytail at release (approximately 230–290 mm) was substantially narrower than for RASU (approximately 350–500 mm), which reduces statistical power to detect a TL effect and limits the biological interpretation of any estimated size relationship (Figure E, Table C).

Despite these constraints, the model results indicate that sex was a less dominant predictor of post-stocking detection probability for Bonytail than was observed for RASU (Table C). This may partly reflect the narrower release size distribution, which would obscure a TL-mediated sex difference, or it may indicate genuinely smaller differences in sex-specific vulnerability for this species. Detection probability at release TL values near the midpoint of the observed range was generally higher for Bonytail than for RASU at the lower end of that species' TL distribution, suggesting that Bonytail stockings did not suffer the extreme early mortality apparent in smaller RASU releases.

Pond-level differences in detection probability were evident in the model estimates, as at the XYTE ponds (Table C). The magnitude and direction of these differences are consistent with variation in scanner coverage and pond characteristics rather than species-specific responses to habitat.

Initial post-stocking detection rates for Bonytail varied by pond and sex (Table D). The overall proportion of fish surviving the 90-day post-stocking period was similar in magnitude to initial RASU stockings, but the subsequent rate of attrition was faster for Bonytail, as discussed in the Known Survivors section below.

---

### Known Survivors

The minimum known survivor trajectories for Bonytail at IPCA Ponds 2, 5, and 6 show a steeper rate of decline among stocked fish than those observed in the RASU ponds (Figure F). However, natural recruitment into the PIT-tagged population has been substantial at all three GIEL ponds, distinguishing them markedly from the RASU ponds 3 and 4 and making the overall trajectory of the PIT-tagged population more complex than a simple stocking-and-attrition curve.

Pond 6 had the most consistent recruitment record, with 311 recruits surviving the 90-day post-tagging period across seven cohort years (2017–2024). Recruitment peaked at 108 fish in 2022, suggesting continued reproductive activity well into the monitoring period. Pond 2 had 394 surviving recruits across five cohort years, with a marked pulse of 162 recruits recorded in 2023 — the largest single-year recruit cohort across any IP pond. Pond 5 had earlier recruitment (84 recruits in 2017–2019) but very little in subsequent years (5 recruits combined in 2022–2023), suggesting that conditions in Pond 5 may have been less favorable for recruitment or juvenile survival in recent years.

Recruits at first capture were substantially smaller than stocked Bonytail (minimum stocking TL ≈ 234 mm; most recruits were under 250 mm, with some as small as 74 mm), confirming these are naturally produced fish tagged during sampling rather than previously stocked adults. Step increases in the survivor trajectories also correspond to supplemental stocking events at ponds that received additional fish, but recruited fish account for a substantial portion of the total PIT-tagged population in Ponds 2 and 6 in recent years.

As with all backwaters in this analysis, transferred fish were excluded from the known survivor count, and fish with suspect transfer records were retained per the criteria described in the Methods section.

---

### Population Estimates

Mark-recapture population estimates for Bonytail at IPCA ponds were computable for fewer census years than for RASU, reflecting the faster attrition of stocked fish and the corresponding reduction in recapture rates in later years (Figure G). In ponds and years where the recapture threshold (R > 3) was not met, the known survivor count is the sole quantitative indicator of population size.

Where estimates were available, they were generally consistent with concurrent known survivor counts. Unlike the RASU ponds 3 and 4, where the PIT-tagged population is driven primarily by stocked fish, the Bonytail ponds have experienced meaningful natural recruitment that partially offsets the loss of stocked fish. Whether that recruitment is sufficient to sustain or grow the PIT-tagged population over the long term is not yet clear from the trajectories alone, particularly given the apparent decline in recruitment at Pond 5 in recent years and the variable year-to-year recruit counts at Ponds 2 and 6. Continued monitoring and supplemental stocking will likely be necessary to ensure viable population sizes are maintained at all three GIEL ponds.
