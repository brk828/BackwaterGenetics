# Summary: Marsh et al. 2024

**Full Citation:**
Marsh, P.C., T.E. Dowling, T.F. Turner, M.J. Osborne, and B.R. Kesner. 2024. Maturation of an off-channel habitat concept to conserve native fishes in the Lower Colorado River. *Monographs of the Western North American Naturalist*, Vol. 15, Article 1. https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/mwnan/vol15/iss1/1

---

## Overview

This monograph reviews two decades of research and management aimed at implementing an off-channel habitat (OCH) conservation strategy for Bonytail (*Gila elegans*; BONY) and Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*; RASU) in the Lower Colorado River. It synthesizes demographic, ecological, and genetic findings from ephemeral and permanent OCHs, proposes a revised and data-driven OCH concept, and evaluates practical challenges to full implementation. The authors conclude that neither species is likely to meet formal ESA recovery criteria in the foreseeable future, but long-term conservation can be achieved through a well-managed OCH network.

---

## Historical Context

- Both BONY and RASU have been under intensive management since the 1980s. BONY is functionally extinct in the wild; RASU populations persist only as repatriated, non-self-sustaining assemblages in most of the Lower Colorado River, with the exception of a limited Lake Mead population.
- Competitive and predatory nonnative fishes are the primary driver of recruitment failure for both species. Hatchery stocking into open waters has consistently failed to establish self-sustaining populations.
- Between 1991 and 2022, approximately 483,000 BONY and 726,000 RASU were stocked into Lower Colorado River habitats with little evidence of long-term establishment in open waters.

---

## Genesis of the OCH Concept

The OCH management framework was formalized in Minckley et al. (2003) in response to decades of failed repatriation into open waters. Core principles:

- Establish self-sustaining, predator-free populations in isolated OCHs where native fish can complete their life cycle.
- Exchange individuals periodically between OCHs and the river mainstem to maintain genetic diversity and prevent divergence.
- Original size recommendation for individual OCHs: 2–5 surface acres (~1–2 ha), considered large enough for viable populations but small enough to manage (drain, renovate, sample).

---

## Research Findings: Ephemeral Pond Studies (Lake Mohave Backwaters)

Studies in isolated ephemeral backwaters adjacent to Lake Mohave (AJ, Dandy for RASU; Nevada Egg, North Nine-Mile for BONY) provided the first direct estimates of demographic and genetic parameters in OCH settings:

- **Sex-biased survival** was consistent across species, years, and backwaters: males stocked at smaller average sizes than females had significantly lower post-stocking detection/harvest rates.
- **Bonytail** demonstrated high Ne/Nc ratios (0.72–0.86) and low variance in reproductive success (VRS), with >85% of adults contributing at least one offspring in most experiments.
- **Razorback sucker** showed more variable Ne/Nc (0.09–0.68) and higher VRS, especially among females. Skewed sex ratios driven by male mortality were a primary factor elevating VRS and reducing Ne/Nc.

---

## Research Findings: Permanent Pond Studies

### Yuma Cove Backwater (RASU)

- 200 RASU (100 of each sex) stocked in 2013. Remote PIT scanning was nearly continuous for 7 years.
- Post-stocking survival was significantly higher for females than males, even after accounting for size differences at release. By February 2014, only 21 of 100 males survived vs. 78 of 100 females.
- By 2016, natural recruitment had replaced stocking-year losses; the population reached ~400 adults and hundreds to thousands of young-of-year.
- Signs of resource limitation (poor survival in 2014–2015 cohorts, negligible YOY survival) emerged within a few years. Approximately 80 adults and 2,877 young-of-year were removed in autumn 2019 to manage density.
- Parentage assignment worked well in 2013 (97% assignment success) but declined in 2014–2015 as recruited fish of shared ancestry complicated analysis.

### Imperial Ponds (RASU and BONY)

- Six ponds at USFWS Imperial National Wildlife Refuge near Yuma, AZ. Originally developed for waterfowl in 2000; used for RASU grow-out 2002–2005; redeveloped and stocked with 150 male + 150 female RASU or BONY per pond in 2017 (3 ponds each species).
- **Pond sizes (BONY ponds):** IP2 = 12.5 acres, IP5 = 21.3 acres, IP6 = 8.8 acres. RASU pond sizes not individually listed in this paper.
- Early stocking (2007) was compromised by failure of the wedge-wire filter to exclude nonnative fish eggs and larvae; nonnatives became established and prevented recruitment by end of 2012.
- Following chemical renovation (2013–2016) and restocking in 2017, recruitment of both species was documented.
- BONY die-offs occurred in IP5 (2020) and IP2 (2022); RASU populations crashed in 2022. These events were attributed to human error or pump failure, not inherent habitat inadequacy.
- BONY reproduction was regular in IP2 and IP6; limited in IP5. For BONY in one pond, high Ne (252, sibship method) and high reproductive success (90–96% of adults contributing offspring) were observed — consistent with ephemeral pond results.
- Reproduction in RASU ponds was more limited and variable for unknown reasons.
- Larger pond size, depth, and shoreline complexity at Imperial Ponds (relative to Yuma Cove and Cibola HLP) may contribute to reduced recruitment even in the absence of nonnative fish.

## Revised OCH Concept

Key revisions to the original Minckley et al. (2003) framework:

1. **Self-sustaining populations** cannot be strictly defined for these species due to stochastic variation in year-class strength; the practical goal is resilient populations that persist and recover from disturbance over long timescales.
2. **Pond size vs. manageability:** No self-sustaining population of BONY or RASU has been documented in an OCH >5 surface acres. Larger ponds are more difficult to monitor, renovate, and manage, and passive water supply (preferred) may preclude draining for renovation.
3. **Genetic management:** VRS should be monitored via parentage-based methods. When Ne/Nc (RASU) or Nb/Nc (BONY) falls below ~0.2, fish should be moved more aggressively among OCHs. As few as 1 migrant per generation can theoretically prevent divergence and inbreeding.
4. **Transfer and harvest:** Regular removal of adults from productive OCHs is needed to prevent overcrowding and maintain age structure. Females should be preferentially removed when sex ratio is female-biased to restore a 1:1 ratio and reduce VRS.
5. **Number of OCHs:** Must be sufficient to buffer against periodic local extirpation. High variance in productivity and extirpation risk across ponds require a larger network to maintain program-wide Ne and abundance.

---

## Recovery vs. Conservation

The authors draw a clear distinction between conservation goals (LCR MSCP, 50-year program) and recovery goals (ESA delisting criteria requiring genetically and demographically viable, self-sustaining populations). Neither species will meet current recovery criteria under foreseeable conditions. The OCH concept provides a realistic path to long-term conservation reliance — managed persistence rather than recovery in the ESA sense.

---

## Future Implementation

- As of 2022, 158 of a planned 360 acres of OCH had been created or protected under the LCR MSCP.
- A new 14-acre pond at Imperial Ponds is planned for 2026.
- A major new facility near Yuma (111 acres across 7 disconnected OCHs plus twelve 1-acre grow-out ponds) was under construction into 2026.
- Key data gaps include: carrying capacity estimates per unit area, causes of variation in productivity among ponds and years, density-dependent effects across life stages, and identification of abiotic/biotic indicators of OCH quality (environmental DNA/metabarcoding cited as a promising approach).
- NGS-based genetic monitoring (GT-seq panels developed for both species) is now available to track genetic metrics (relatedness, Ne, VRS) with greater resolution than previous microsatellite approaches.

## Relevance to BackwaterGenetics Project

- Primary reference for **Imperial Ponds site history, design rationale, and size** (BONY pond sizes: IP2 = 12.5 ac, IP5 = 21.3 ac, IP6 = 8.8 ac).
- Provides context for the **sex-biased post-stocking survival** patterns documented in RASU and BONY analyses — the pattern is robust across habitats and years and is partially (but not entirely) explained by size at release.
- Documents the **Yuma Cove backwater population trajectory** through ~2021, serving as a pre-2022 baseline for demographic parameters compared to the current BackwaterGenetics analyses.
- Supports interpretation of **variable natural recruitment** among IP ponds and between species: BONY tends to have lower VRS and higher Ne/Nc; RASU reproductive success is more variable and sensitive to sex ratio skew.
- The proposed threshold of **Ne/Nc < 0.2** (or Nb/Nc < 0.2) as a trigger for management action (fish movement) provides context for interpreting genetic monitoring outputs from the Dowling lab.
